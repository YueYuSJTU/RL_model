### Comment 1 — definition of $I_x, I_y, I_z$ (R1-1)

> In Equation (3), the quantities $I_x, I_y, I_z$ must be clearly defined.

**Response.** We agree that the inertia quantities were used without being defined. They are now defined from first principles, so that the coefficients $C_1$–$C_9$ are fully determined by the rigid-body inertia properties of the aircraft and can be recomputed by the reader. Specifically:

* new Eq. (3) gives the body-fixed frame $Ox_By_Bz_B$ (origin at the centre of gravity, $x_B$ forward, $y_B$ to the right wing, $z_B$ downward) and the inertia tensor $\mathbf{J}$ expressed in it, with the sign convention for the off-diagonal entries made explicit;
* new Eq. (4) defines the moments of inertia as $I_{xx}=\int (y^2+z^2)\,\mathrm{d}m$, $I_{yy}=\int (x^2+z^2)\,\mathrm{d}m$, $I_{zz}=\int (x^2+y^2)\,\mathrm{d}m$;
* new Eq. (5) defines the products of inertia $I_{xy}=\int xy\,\mathrm{d}m$, $I_{xz}=\int xz\,\mathrm{d}m$, $I_{yz}=\int yz\,\mathrm{d}m$.

The text states that all six quantities are taken about the centre of gravity and have units of $\mathrm{slug}\cdot\mathrm{ft}^2$; that the lateral symmetry of the aircraft about the $x_B$–$z_B$ plane makes $I_{xy}$ and $I_{yz}$ vanish, so that $I_{xz}$ is the only non-zero off-diagonal entry; and that $\mathbf{J}$ is symmetric positive definite, so that $\Gamma=I_{xx}I_{zz}-I_{xz}^2>0$. The numerical values are also given, together with the loading state to which they correspond, and the dependence on the fuel state is quantified.

**Changes.** Section 2.2: the paragraph preceding Eq. (6) (formerly Eq. (3) of the submission) and Eqs. (3)–(5); Eq. (7) gives $I_{xx}=1.229\times10^{4}$, $I_{yy}=5.711\times10^{4}$, $I_{zz}=6.707\times10^{4}$ and $I_{xz}=1.060\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{slug}\cdot\mathrm{ft}^{2}$ at the engagement loading state (3000 lb of fuel, total weight $20{,}630$ lb, $m=641.2$ slugs), and the text gives $I_{xx}=9.50\times10^{3}$, $I_{yy}=5.682\times10^{4}$, $I_{zz}=6.411\times10^{4}$, $I_{xz}=9.46\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{slug}\cdot\mathrm{ft}^{2}$ at fuel exhaustion (17,630 lb, $m=548.0$ slugs). ### Comment 2 — general threshold variables and reward-parameter assignment (R1-2)

> In Equation (5), the specific values 500 and 15° should be replaced by general threshold variables, which should be assigned numerical values with proper physical units only after the subsequent analysis. Similarly, the parameters in Equation (13), Table 2, and Table 3 also appear overly heuristic. The authors are advised to first analyse the composition of the reward function and then assign values to these variables based on a set of generalised threshold variables; otherwise, the current discussion is insufficient.

**Response.** We have restructured both the engagement model and the reward description along exactly the lines suggested.

*(a) Weapon engagement zone.* The WEZ indicator (now Eq. (9)) is now written with the general threshold variables $D_{\min}$, $D_{\max}$ and $\theta_{\max}$; the text defines what each of them means (near and far boundary of the effective firing range, maximum admissible off-boresight angle) and states explicitly that they describe the geometry of a valid firing solution rather than free parameters of the learning problem. Only afterwards are they assigned values with units, $D_{\min}=500$ ft, $D_{\max}=4500$ ft and $\theta_{\max}=15^\circ$, each with its physical justification: the near boundary is the minimum separation at which a tracking solution can still be established and rules out a point-blank firing condition, the far boundary is the range beyond which gun dispersion and loss of lead accuracy make hits improbable at the closing speeds of the modelled aircraft, and the cone angle follows the ADT criterion. Re-deriving these thresholds from the scenario also revealed that the previous far boundary (3000 ft) was inconsistent with the normalisation of the damage rate; the damage law is therefore now written as $\lambda(D)=\lambda_0\,(D_{\max}-D)/(D_{\max}-D_{\min})$ (Eq. (10)) with $\lambda_0=1$ HP/s, so that it vanishes at the far boundary by construction, and the initial health $\mathrm{HP}_0=3$ is stated so that $\lambda_0$ can be interpreted (a target held at minimum range is destroyed within 3 s).

*(b) Reward functions.* The two reward tables (now Tables 3 and 4) no longer contain numerical constants: every term is given in a general form with its symbolic weight $w_\bullet$, scaling factor $s_\bullet$ or slope $k_\bullet$, the notation for the thresholds is defined below the table, and each term is discussed in terms of what requirement of the task it represents and how its parameters act. Equation (13) of the submission (the scaled asymptotic error form, now Eq. (17)) is likewise written with the general weight $w_i$ and scaling factor $s_i$, and the two range-weighting functions used by the aiming terms are now defined explicitly as band-pass functions with near and far logistic boundaries (Eq. (15)), which was previously implicit. A new table (Table 5) collects the terminal reward terms, which were not listed before.

The numerical values are assigned afterwards, in a new subsection, *Reward Composition and Parameter Calibration* (§3.2.2), in which the parameters are explicitly divided into two groups: (i) the thresholds and scaling factors that describe the physical setting (gun-envelope boundaries, alignment thresholds and range bands, the reference condition of trimmed flight, the safety altitude), which are fixed by the engagement scenario; and (ii) the weights $w_\bullet$ and slopes $k_\bullet$ of the shaping terms, which interact and whose values are meaningful only relative to one another, so that they cannot be obtained in closed form. For this second group the calibration procedure is documented: the diagnostics used (per-term reward averages recorded separately for every component, win/draw/loss rates, the fraction of engagement time spent inside the WEZ, the mean track angle, and the crash rate), the direction of each adjustment and the observation that triggered it, and the acceptance rule (an adjustment was retained only when it improved the outcome statistics without increasing the crash rate or degrading the flight quality, and was reverted otherwise). The values of every parameter are then listed, together with the reasoning that fixes each of them, and we state explicitly that they reflect the balance between the terms rather than a unique optimum, and that they are reported in full so that the reward structure can be reproduced.

### Comment 3 — quantification of opponent strength and baseline characterization of the benchmark opponents (R1-3)

> The formation of the sparse reward is related to the method used for evaluating the initial task opponents. However, the proposed scheme for quantifying opponent strength relies too heavily on the training capability itself and is essentially not sufficient to support the claimed quantitative relationship. Moreover, the authors need to provide an in-depth analysis of the baseline characteristics of the opponents, even if those opponents are generated randomly. It is recommended to construct a benchmark strategy table for comparison.

**Response.** We accept this criticism and have removed the two circular elements it identifies.

*(a) Opponent characterization.* The benchmark opponents are no longer described by qualitative labels ("aggressive", "evasive", "random-manoeuvring") or by their strength relative to the learning agent. Each of the eight policies is now characterized by direct measurement of its own flight behaviour, under a stimulus that is identical for all of them and that involves neither reward nor engagement outcome: every policy is flown for 60 s from the same initial condition with its own control commands, while the opposing aircraft is a scripted constant-command trim drone common to all eight episodes. Table 6 (the requested benchmark strategy table) reports five measured quantities — mean bank angle, mean climb rate, mean elevator command, mean speed and mean closure rate towards the other aircraft — and five behaviour classes are derived from them, with the measured signature of each class stated in the text: sustained maximum-manoeuvre flight (86.3° mean bank, saturated nose-up elevator, +113.8 ft/s), high-manoeuvre energy trade (60.1° mean bank with the lowest speed and specific energy), moderate manoeuvre (47.8°), steady descent (−374.4 ft/s, the only policy that loses altitude throughout) and near-level cruise flight (mean bank 3.2°–24.2°, elevator within 0.17 of trim). Two properties of the pool are then stated explicitly, because they bound what can be claimed from it: the pool is strongly asymmetric in manoeuvre intensity, so that any single aggregate over the eight opponents mixes two very different regimes; and none of the eight opponents directs its manoeuvring at the other aircraft in this measurement (the mean closure rate is negative for all eight, and the fraction of turning explained by the line-of-sight angle to the other aircraft does not exceed $R^2=0.11$ for any of them, with five below 0.02). We state that this is consistent with the reward terms on which these policies were trained, and that the pool is therefore best read as a ladder of flight behaviour rather than of adversarial pressure. This is deliberately a weaker claim than the one made in the original submission, and it is the claim the measurements support.

*(b) Model-comparison metrics.* The evaluation metrics of the five compared models have been changed so that the comparison does not depend on quantities that the reward optimizes. The previous metrics — mean track angle, gun-opportunity ratio and total damage to the opponent — are respectively the argument of the aiming reward terms, the indicator function of the damage mechanism itself, and a monotone function of a terminal reward term, so that they measure how well a policy optimizes its own objective rather than an independent capability. They are replaced by two quantities that appear in no term of any reward: the mean specific-energy margin over the opponent, $\Delta E_s$, which is the quantity that an energy fight seeks to obtain, and the line-of-sight turning figure $R^2$, the squared correlation between the line-of-sight angle to the opponent and the signed turn rate, which measures the fraction of a policy's turning that is explained by the position of the adversary and therefore separates a policy that manoeuvres in response to the opponent from one that follows a trajectory of its own. Both are measured under the same protocol as the win-rate statistics (randomized initial states, $8\times150$ engagements per model).

The two metrics separate the five models in the same way as the outcome statistics: $\Delta E_s$ increases monotonically with the win rate, from $-2941$ ft for the baseline to $+4679$ ft for the proposed LSTM-based model, which is the only model that finishes all eight matchups with a positive margin (its smallest value is $+2955$ ft), whereas the baseline finishes six of the eight with a negative margin; and the line-of-sight figure separates the two variants of the proposed method ($R^2=0.35$ and $0.36$) from the other three models ($0.22$–$0.24$). We also amended the claim on the average specific energy in the cross-play analysis so that it is stated as an energy margin over the opponent and is consistent with the engagement-level figure of Table 7.

### Comment 4 — separate treatment of opponent-pool initialization and of the goal-point model (R1-4)

> A dynamic opponent pool is a common and necessary training technique, but most existing works only present the design scheme. The Opponent Pool Initialization, as the core component for handling generalisation capability, should be discussed and tested separately. In addition, the parameters of the goalpoint model and its necessity warrant further investigation.

**Response.** We have added a dedicated ablation of the pool initialization and a separate analysis of the goal-point model.

*(a) Opponent-pool initialization.* A new section (§4.5) isolates the contribution of the initialization from all the other components of the framework. Two arms are trained that differ only in whether the pool is seeded with externally pretrained opponents, everything else being held fixed: in the *with-initialization* arm the pool is seeded with a goal-point navigation policy together with two combat-capable policies derived from it, all three protected, leaving one refreshable slot (this reproduces the configuration of the proposed method); in the *without-initialization* arm the pool contains a single model, namely the arm's own stage-1 checkpoint, with no protected opponent, so that all four slots are refreshable from the first update onwards. Each arm is trained for $3.0\times10^{8}$ steps and is then evaluated on the benchmark pool with 1000 engagements per opponent.

The two arms differ both in how long the return keeps improving and in the level at which it settles. With initialization the return rises from 195 (window ending at $1.5\times10^7$ steps) to about 1250 at $6.5\times10^7$ steps — a factor of more than six over the first $8\times10^7$ steps — and stays at a comparable or higher level afterwards. Without initialization the return reaches 539 in the window ending at $2\times10^7$ steps, which remains its highest value of the whole run, shows no further improvement after $5\times10^7$ steps, and declines towards the end of training; over the last $10^8$ steps the mean evaluation return is 744 with initialization against 275 without, a factor of about 2.7. On the benchmark, the win rate is 42.49% with initialization against 14.44% without, and the same separation appears in every secondary metric: the gun-opportunity ratio falls from 0.156 to 0.078 and the mean health margin from $+1.80$ to $+0.99$ HP. The breakdown by opponent type localizes the failure: the without-initialization arm still wins 25.1% against the four level-flight opponents but only 6.7% against the two early-combat opponents and 1.6% against the goal-point policy, that is, precisely against the opponents that manoeuvre in order to deny a firing solution, whereas with initialization the same sequence only degrades from 66.6% to 30.6% and 5.0%. A direct comparison between the two arms, without involving the benchmark, points in the same direction: over 300 engagements in each direction, the with-initialization arm wins 4.0% and loses none, with a mean health margin of $+0.81$, while the without-initialization arm wins none of the 300 engagements in the opposite direction, finishes at $-0.83$ HP and obtains about one seventh of the gun-opportunity time of its opponent.

We also report the refresh statistics of the pool, which show *why* the arm without initialization fails rather than only that it does. In that arm 108 challenger evaluations were performed during training, the challenger achieved a mean win rate of 0.26%, 99.7% of the engagements ended in a draw, and only four evaluations contained any engagement that the challenger won; the pool was nevertheless refreshed 27 times, because the update rule also rewards small differences in remaining health, whose mean value was $+0.03$ HP. The final pool of that arm plays 100% draws against itself, with almost identical remaining health on both sides (2.74 against 2.77) and a gun-opportunity ratio of 1.9%. These numbers are consistent with a pool that progressively loses its ability to discriminate between policies and with a policy that settles into an equilibrium in which engagements rarely produce a decision. The conclusion we draw is stated in the paper: in the setting studied here, the initialization of the pool has a substantial influence on the outcome of training, and curriculum learning together with the periodic refresh did not compensate for the absence of an external anchor.

*(b) Necessity and parameters of the goal-point model.* A new paragraph in §3.3.1 explains why the goal-point model is used at all. In the conventional approach, every opponent with a distinct manoeuvring style has to be specified by its own reward function and produced by its own training run, so that enlarging the opponent set costs one additional reward design and one training run per style, and the resulting policies remain unrelated to one another. In the goal-point model the target point enters the policy as a conditional input rather than as part of its learning objective; changing the way the target point is generated therefore changes the manoeuvring habit of the same policy with no modification of the reward function and no retraining. The paper gives concrete examples of this re-parameterization (a target point drawn at random yields a non-adversarial opponent; a target point overwritten in real time with the position of the ego aircraft turns the same policy into an aggressive pursuer; a target point moving along a line, along a perturbed path or along a helix drives the same policy into sustained banked turns, weaving and altitude changes), and states that the threat level of an opponent is thereby a change of one input variable rather than a new reward design followed by a new training run.

The parameters of the model are now reported in full: the general form (Eq. (17)) and its interpretation (each component equals its weight when the error vanishes, retains half of that value when the error equals the scaling factor, and decays smoothly for larger errors), the distance term (Eq. (18), $w_{\mathrm{dist}}=4.0$, $s_{\mathrm{dist}}=2000$ ft, with the reason for the scaling factor — it is comparable to the separation at which target points are generated), the heading term (Eq. (19), $w_{\mathrm{head}}=0.2$, $s_{\mathrm{head}}=0.2$ rad $\approx11.5^\circ$, with the resulting range of the term and its relation to the distance term), the closure-rate error term with its target value $V_c^{\ast}=700$ ft/s and scaling factor, the aileron-magnitude, action-smoothness and low-altitude terms with their weights and windows, and the terminal condition (the target point is considered to be reached when the distance falls below 500 ft).

**Changes.** Section 3.3.1: the new paragraph on the necessity of the goal-point model and the parameter values of Eqs. (17)–(19) and of the four auxiliary reward terms. Section 4.5 (new): *Opponent Pool Initialization Ablation*, with Figure 13 (training stability of the two arms), Table 8 (outcome distribution and air-combat metrics), Table 9 (win rate by opponent type), Figure 14 (per-opponent outcomes) and the analysis of the pool-refresh statistics. Conclusion: the initialization finding is now stated as one of the two main findings of the study.

### Comment 3 — the new finding beyond integrating known ideas (R2-3)

> The Introduction already discusses previous curriculum and population-based approaches, including references [19]–[26]. The manuscript should identify what new finding follows from this study beyond integrating those ideas in the chosen simulator.

**Response.** We agree that the original manuscript described the combination of known techniques without stating what the study adds to them. The revised Conclusion identifies two findings that we consider transferable, and both are supported by experiments that were added in this revision rather than by the integration itself.

First, the value of an opponent pool depends on how it is initialized, and the failure mode of a pool that is initialized only from the agent's own early snapshot is measurable rather than a matter of degree. With the pool seeded from the arm's own stage-1 checkpoint and with no protected opponent, the refresh comparisons lose their discriminating power (108 challenger evaluations, mean challenger win rate 0.26%, 99.7% of engagements drawn, only four evaluations containing any win by the challenger, and 27 refreshes driven by a mean health difference of $+0.03$ HP), the evaluation return stops improving after the first few cycles and settles at a factor of about 2.7 below the initialized arm, and the resulting policy loses 28.1 percentage points of benchmark win rate, with the degradation concentrated on the opponents that manoeuvre to deny a firing solution (6.7% against the early-combat opponents and 1.6% against the goal-point policy, against 30.6% and 5.0% with initialization). Both findings are stated in the Conclusion together with the concrete results of the framework (55.6% against 0.42% win rate, and the reward-independent capability metrics of Table 7), so that the contribution of the paper is expressed in terms of what was learned about the training procedure rather than in terms of the simulator in which it was instantiated.

### Comment 4 — the computational-efficiency claim (R2-4)

> The computational-efficiency claim is insufficiently specified.

**Response.** The claim has been replaced by a controlled measurement and an analysis of its origin. The following information is now given.

*What is measured.* A benchmark that drives the environment from a fixed, seeded action stream while the opponent is driven by a policy checkpoint from an opponent pool — that is, exactly the work the environment performs during rollout collection, with no PPO, reward normalization or evaluation callback involved. All compared implementations are driven from the same initial-condition distribution, the same opponent pool and the same command sequence, so that the sampling architecture is the only difference between them.

*How it is measured.* Every entry of Table 2 is the median of three runs of 20,000 decision steps following a warm-up of 200 steps, with 30 dynamics processes where applicable, on the workstation described in §4.1 (Intel Core i9-14900KF, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti). Both units are reported in order to remove any ambiguity about what a step is: the environment integrates the flight dynamics at 60 Hz while the agent interacts with it at 10 Hz, so one decision step comprises six integration steps, and decision steps per second and integration steps per second are given side by side. Three implementations are compared: a serial single-process implementation (one forward pass per decision step), a serial implementation in which each of the 30 workers runs its own instance of the same per-step inference, and the proposed decoupled sampling.

*What the numbers are.* At an equal number of processes (30), decoupling raises the aggregate sampling rate from 2343 to 8396 decision steps per second, that is by a factor of 3.6, and against the single-process serial implementation, which is the limiting case of the same serialization, the factor is 6.8 (1228 decision steps per second, 7367 integration steps per second, against 8396 and 50,378 for the proposed architecture). The previous statement of "50 to 6700 simulation time steps per second, a 134× speedup", which mixed units and used a comparison that was not like-for-like, has been removed.

*Where the gain comes from.* The improvement is not obtained by adding arithmetic capacity: a single forward pass through the opponent policy performs approximately $1.5\times10^6$ floating-point operations, so that even at the highest measured rate the aggregate inference load remains of the order of $10^{10}$ FLOP s$^{-1}$, more than three orders of magnitude below the arithmetic capability of the GPU. The serialized implementations are limited instead by the latency of short and frequent calls — launching the inference, synchronizing the worker threads and transferring tensors between host and device at every decision step — while the CPU cores are occupied by flight-dynamics integration. Collecting the observations of all sub-environments into a single batch replaces thirty such calls per parallel step with one, so that the overhead is amortized over the whole batch and the batch is large enough to occupy the arithmetic units of the accelerator. The flight dynamics themselves are parallelized over processes rather than over threads, because the JSBSim integration is a scalar, branch-intensive workload that does not release the interpreter lock. The decoupling therefore converts a latency-bound sampling procedure into a throughput-bound one.

**Changes.** Section 2.3: the claim is replaced by a description of the measurement, Table 2 with the three implementations and the two units, and the analysis above; §4.1 already describes the workstation on which the measurement was taken. The benchmark itself (`scripts/benchmark/bench_env_throughput.py`, together with the legacy serial wrapper `scripts/benchmark/self_play_wrapper_legacy.py`) is part of the code base of this work and can be provided with the revision; it records the full protocol (environment id, step counts, warm-up, repeats, device, CPU topology and library versions) with every result.
